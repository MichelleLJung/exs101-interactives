Reaction Time, Attention & Movement

Hick's Law · Stroop Effect · Fitts's Law

How to use this lab

Complete three short experiments on the same device. First predict what will happen, complete practice and recorded trials, then explain your own results. Allow approximately 30–40 minutes.

Before you begin: Use a laptop or desktop with a mouse or trackpad if possible. Keep your device and input method consistent. Browser timing and device differences mean these are demonstrations, not clinical measurements. Do not compare your exact times with classmates.

Your data stays in this browser. Use “Save progress” if you need to return later; “Load progress” restores that file. No data is uploaded to a server.

What you are investigating

Hick's Law: More possible responses generally increase response-selection time. Stroop Effect: Conflicting information can interfere with selective attention. Fitts's Law: Small or distant targets generally require more movement time for accurate pointing.

1 · Hick's Law

Reaction time is the interval from a stimulus appearing to the start of your response. Simple reaction time uses one known response; choice reaction time requires selecting among alternatives.

Think first: When only one response is possible, you react without choosing. With 2, 4, or 8 possibilities, you must identify the number and select its matching response. Check: Which condition measures simple reaction time? The 1-choice condition.
Follow these steps
  1. Predict how reaction time will change with more choices.
  2. Use the numbered buttons displayed on the screen.
  3. Keep your pointer ready, but wait for the number to appear. Clicking early causes a false start and restarts the attempt.
  4. When a number appears, click the matching button. For the one-choice condition, click 1.
  5. Complete 2 practice responses, followed automatically by 8 recorded attempts. Wrong answers count as attempts but are excluded from the average time. The next number appears automatically after each response.
  6. At the end of each condition, select Begin Next Condition. After all four, continue to Stroop or open Results.

Remember: The waiting period is not part of your reaction time. The response buttons remain visible during the waiting period. Clicking before the number appears is a false start; that attempt restarts without counting.

2 · Stroop Effect

Selective attention means focusing on task-relevant information while ignoring distractions. Interference occurs when competing information makes the task harder.

Think first: Reading a familiar word can happen automatically. Your task is to suppress that word response and identify the ink color. Check: If BLUE is printed in red ink, the correct answer is RED.
Follow these steps
  1. Predict whether conflicting word meanings will slow you down.
  2. Identify the ink color, not the word you read.
  3. Select the on-screen button that matches the ink color.
  4. Complete 3 practice responses, followed automatically by 16 recorded attempts (incorrect answers count as attempts but are excluded from the average time) (8 matching and 8 conflicting) in a mixed sequence.
  5. The next word appears automatically. When finished, continue to Fitts's Law or open Results.

Example: BLUE written in red ink → respond RED.

3 · Fitts's Law

Movement time is the time spent executing a movement. Speed–accuracy tradeoff: moving faster can increase misses, particularly with small targets. Target distance and width both affect pointing difficulty.

Think first: A farther target requires a longer movement, while a smaller target requires greater pointing precision. Check: This task measures movement time, not the delay before responding to a stimulus.
Follow these steps
  1. Predict which combination of target size and distance will take longest.
  2. Use a mouse or trackpad. Select Begin Practice once.
  3. Move your pointer to START and press it. Keep the pointer moving toward TARGET, then release on TARGET. Timing covers that pointing movement.
  4. Repeat for 2 practice movements and 8 recorded movements per condition. After each attempt, return to START. The recorded trials begin automatically after practice; no extra Begin click is needed.
  5. A miss counts as an unsuccessful attempt. Try again. After each set of 8 successful movements, select Begin Next Condition.
  6. When all four conditions are complete, open Results & PDF.

Important: Faster is not always better if accuracy decreases. Use the same device throughout.

How to read your results

ms means milliseconds. 1,000 ms = 1 second. 245 ms = 0.245 seconds; 745 ms = 0.745 seconds. For reaction and movement time, smaller numbers mean faster performance. For accuracy, a larger number of correct responses means fewer errors.

Compare conditions within each experiment. Faster does not always mean better: check correct responses and missed targets alongside times. The graph shows time (higher = slower); it does not show accuracy.
